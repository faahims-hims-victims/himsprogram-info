When Oversight Disappears: What Legal Scholarship Reveals About the FAA’s HIMS Program

Published by Pilots for HIMS Reform

In 2026, a law review article published by the University of Oklahoma College of Law in the Oklahoma Law Review examined a growing concern in professional licensing: monitoring programs operating without transparency, oversight, or meaningful accountability.

As published legal scholarship, the article reflects a broader academic examination of how professional oversight systems can evolve beyond their intended scope when transparency and accountability are limited.

Although the article focused on Lawyer Assistance Programs (LAPs) used in bar admissions, its conclusions extend far beyond the legal profession. The structure, authority, and risks it describes closely mirror what aviation professionals have experienced for years within the FAA’s Human Intervention Motivation Study (HIMS) program.

The parallels are difficult to ignore—and they raise serious legal and ethical questions.

A System Designed to Help—But Lacking Guardrails

LAPs were originally created as supportive, confidential resources for lawyers dealing with substance use or mental health challenges. Over time, however, their role expanded.

Today, as the article explains, these programs often:

  • Define what it means to be “fit” to practice
  • Recommend or impose treatment requirements
  • Enforce long-term monitoring agreements
  • Operate with little transparency or external oversight

Critically, the same entity can evaluate, prescribe, and monitor—effectively consolidating authority that would normally be separated in a regulated system.

The article warns that this structure creates the conditions for overreach, particularly when there are limited mechanisms for review or appeal.

The HIMS Parallel

While the LAP system applies to lawyers, the FAA’s HIMS program operates in a strikingly similar way within aviation.

Under HIMS, pilots may be required to:

  • Undergo psychiatric or substance use evaluations
  • Participate in prescribed treatment programs
  • Enter long-term monitoring agreements
  • Submit to repeated testing and reporting requirements

Like LAPs, the HIMS framework often relies on a network of designated evaluators and program-affiliated physicians who play a central role in determining outcomes.

And like LAPs, these decisions can have profound consequences—not just for certification, but for a pilot’s career, financial stability, and long-term livelihood.

A Lack of Transparency

One of the most concerning findings highlighted in the published legal scholarship is the absence of transparency.

In LAP systems:

  • Conditional admission decisions are confidential
  • Monitoring agreements are not publicly available
  • Outcome data is limited or nonexistent
  • Judicial review is rare or practically inaccessible

This creates what the article describes as a “black box”—a system where decisions are made without visibility into how or why they are reached.

The same concerns arise in the HIMS context.

Pilots often have limited access to:

  • The underlying rationale for decisions
  • The standards being applied
  • Any meaningful appeal process

Without transparency, accountability becomes difficult—and consistency becomes impossible to verify.

When Treatment Becomes a Requirement

The article also highlights cases in which individuals were required to:

  • Enter inpatient treatment programs costing tens of thousands of dollars
  • Cease medically prescribed treatments
  • Agree to multi-year monitoring conditions

These requirements were imposed even in situations where:

  • Individuals demonstrated long-term stability
  • Treating physicians supported continued care
  • There was no evidence of current impairment

This raises an important question:

At what point does a support system become a coercive one?

Legal Implications

Due Process
Professional licenses are widely recognized as a protected property interest. When a system imposes significant restrictions without clear standards, transparency, or meaningful review, it raises fundamental due process concerns.

Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA)
The ADA prohibits discrimination based on disability, including substance use disorders and mental health conditions. Regulatory decisions must be based on individualized assessments—not generalized assumptions or diagnoses alone.

The article specifically warns that systems like LAPs risk:

  • Imposing burdens based on diagnosis rather than conduct
  • Relying on stereotypes rather than evidence
  • Requiring unnecessary or excessive conditions

Delegation of Authority
Another concern is whether regulatory bodies are effectively delegating decision-making authority to third-party programs that operate outside traditional oversight structures.

When external actors define standards, impose conditions, and control outcomes, questions arise about the limits of that delegation.

Coercion and “Voluntary” Participation
Programs may be described as voluntary—but when declining participation results in loss of certification or employment, the line between voluntary and coercive becomes blurred.

These concerns are not unique to aviation, but part of a broader legal discussion about how professional licensing systems must balance public safety with individual rights.

A Broader Pattern

Perhaps the most important takeaway is that this is not an isolated issue.

The article makes clear that these structural concerns are not limited to one profession, are not limited to one state, and reflect a broader pattern in professional regulation.

When systems combine expansive authority, limited oversight, and confidential decision-making, they create environments where even well-intentioned programs can drift beyond their original purpose.

The Need for Oversight and Reform

The goal of programs like LAPs and HIMS is important. Supporting professionals in recovery and ensuring safety are legitimate and necessary objectives.

But the structure of these programs must reflect the same principles expected of any regulatory system:

  • Transparency
  • Accountability
  • Consistency
  • Respect for individual rights

As the legal scholarship suggests, reform should focus on:

  • Clearly defined and objective fitness standards
  • Independent review mechanisms
  • Transparency in decision-making and outcomes
  • Alignment with ADA requirements
  • Separation of evaluation, treatment, and monitoring roles

A Question That Cannot Be Ignored

If a system can define fitness, impose treatment, control a person’s ability to work, and operate without transparency—what limits exist on its authority?

Moving Forward

The concerns raised in this legal scholarship provide an opportunity—not just for reflection, but for meaningful improvement.

By examining these issues across professions, we can better understand where systems succeed, where they fall short, and how they can be strengthened.

For aviation professionals, the parallels are clear. The challenge now is to ensure that the systems designed to support safety and recovery do so in a way that is fair, transparent, and grounded in sound legal and medical principles.

Source

Schaefer, Paula. Conditional Admission and the Hazards of Lawyer Assistance Programs Operating Without Transparency or Oversight. Oklahoma Law Review (2026).
