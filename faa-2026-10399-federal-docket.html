Official Federal Docket Now Live

It's Official: HIMS Reform Is Now on the Federal Docket

The FAA has accepted Pilots for HIMS Reform's petition for rulemaking for review. Now the aviation community has a place to put its voice into the federal record.

FAA-2026-10399
This is the moment to speak

Read It. Comment. Share It.

Regulations.gov is currently displaying a Comment button for the P4HR petition. Go directly to the federal docket, read the petition, and put your experience and views into the record.

COMMENT ON FAA-2026-10399 READ FAA ACKNOWLEDGEMENT

There are moments in an advocacy movement when something changes. This is one of them.

For more than a year, Pilots for HIMS Reform has researched, documented, written, questioned, challenged, testified, met, filed, published, and pushed for something the aviation community has been told for far too long could not — or would not — change.

Today, our demand for reform is no longer simply sitting on a P4HR webpage.

It is sitting on a United States government website.

It has a federal docket number.

And the Federal Aviation Administration has formally accepted our petition for review.

Regulatory Docket No. FAA-2026-10399 is live.

The FAA Has Formally Acknowledged the Petition

On August 24, 2026, the FAA's Office of Rulemaking sent Pilots for HIMS Reform an acknowledgement letter regarding our August 20 petition for rulemaking.

The first sentence matters:

FAA Office of Rulemaking “The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) has received and begun processing your petition for rulemaking.”

The FAA assigned the matter Regulatory Docket No. FAA-2026-10399.

More importantly, after its initial review, the FAA told P4HR:

FAA Initial Review “Your petition contains sufficient information to be accepted by the FAA for review.”

That is an important procedural milestone.

It does not mean the FAA has granted the petition. It does not mean the reforms we have requested are already becoming regulations.

But it means something very real:

The petition made it through the door.

The FAA has accepted it for review, begun processing it, and placed the matter into an official federal regulatory docket.

Look at What Is Now Before the FAA

The FAA's own acknowledgement summarizes just how broad this petition is.

According to the agency, the proposed rulemaking addresses: special issuance certification, HIMS monitoring, substance-related and psychiatric aeromedical review, independent medical evaluation, agency decision deadlines, and transition to unrestricted medical certification.

Those are not peripheral questions. They go directly to the structure of the FAA aeromedical system and to issues pilots and their families have been raising for years:

Who decides when monitoring ends?
What objective standards govern that decision?
How long should the FAA be permitted to take?
When does demonstrated recovery become enough?
What independent review exists when an airman disputes a medical judgment?
How much discretion should one evaluator have over another person's career?
What standards should govern psychiatric and substance-related decisions?
When has a pilot earned the right to return to unrestricted certification?

P4HR's petition asks the FAA to address those questions through actual regulation rather than leaving enormous portions of this system dependent upon unwritten practices, subjective interpretation, and administrative discretion.

Please Don't Waste Your Comment

We want numbers.

But we also want substance.

The strongest possible federal record will not be hundreds of identical copied-and-pasted sentences. It will be pilots, aviation professionals, physicians, families, and others independently explaining what they have experienced, what they have observed, and what reforms they believe are needed.

Tell the FAA what you have actually seen.

  • Tell them what worked — and what did not.
  • Tell them about unexplained delays.
  • Tell them about years of successful recovery.
  • Tell them about indefinite or extended monitoring.
  • Tell them about the financial and professional costs.
  • Tell them if the system created fear around seeking treatment.
  • Tell them about arbitrary or inconsistent requirements.
  • Tell them about medical professionals constrained by the system.
  • Tell them whether independent medical review would improve fairness.
  • Tell them why objective standards could improve aviation safety.

And if you disagree with some portion of our petition, tell them that too.

We want a serious regulatory conversation.

A public record demonstrating that aviation professionals across the country are paying attention to these issues is much harder to ignore than complaints made quietly and individually behind closed doors.

This Is Bigger Than P4HR

Ultimately, this petition is not about whether the FAA agrees with Pilots for HIMS Reform.

It is about whether America's aeromedical regulatory system should be capable of explaining its decisions.

It is about whether medical monitoring should have identifiable endpoints.

It is about whether two similarly situated pilots should receive similar treatment.

It is about whether disputed medical judgments should be independently reviewable.

It is about whether standards affecting careers, families, health, and livelihoods should be written down.

And it is about whether aviation safety is best served when pilots trust the medical system enough to seek help — or when they fear what may happen if they do.

Those questions belong to the entire aviation community.

Now they are before the FAA.

From a Coalition to the Federal Record

When Pilots for HIMS Reform began, there was no guarantee anyone would listen.

There still is not.

There is no guarantee this petition will be granted. There is no guarantee the FAA will agree with us.

The FAA has already informed P4HR that, because of the complexity of the request, it currently expects its decision on the petition to take longer than 120 days.

Fine.

We have been told to wait before.

We are not going anywhere.

The difference today is that there is now an official place in the federal government's own regulatory system where this fight exists.
